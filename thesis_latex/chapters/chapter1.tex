\section{Background of the Study}

\subsection{Global Context: Digital Transformation in Industrial Parks}

Industrial park management is undergoing a profound digital shift worldwide. Parks increasingly rely on smart technologies — not just for operational efficiency, but to give their tenant enterprises a genuine competitive edge. Central to this transformation are integrated digital platforms that consolidate administrative services, enable real-time communication, and support data-driven decisions \citep{Yang2025, Huang2023}.

China stands out as a clear frontrunner. Chinese parks have built comprehensive digital ecosystems — largely powered by WeChat Work and DingTalk — that fundamentally reshape how parks and tenants interact. Through these platforms, tenants access business registration, permit processing, facility management, and stakeholder communication from a single interface \citep{Huang2023, Schreieck2022}. The result is a compelling demonstration of how digital governance can simultaneously streamline park operations and improve the tenant experience.

Naturally, the success of these models has sparked interest in replicating them elsewhere, particularly in developing economies seeking to strengthen the competitiveness of their industrial zones. The Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) and its digital counterpart, the Digital Silk Road (DSR), have created concrete pathways for technology exchange between China and partner countries across Africa \citep{LiuBRI2023, deKluiver2024, ElKadi2024}.

\subsection{Ethiopian Context: Industrial Parks and Digital Transformation}

Ethiopia has placed industrial park development at the centre of its economic transformation strategy \citep{Oqubay2015}. The Industrial Parks Development Corporation (IPDC), established in 2014, manages a growing portfolio of parks including Bole Lemi Industrial Park, Eastern Industrial Zone, Hawassa Industrial Park, and others \citep{IPDC2020}. These parks host domestic and international manufacturers and play a meaningful role in generating export revenue and employment \citep{Guteta2024, Tesfaw2023}.

The China-Ethiopia relationship in this space is especially notable. Several Ethiopian industrial parks were built with Chinese investment and technical support, and Chinese firms constitute a significant share of tenants. This relationship creates both a practical opportunity and a strong justification for examining how Chinese digital governance approaches might be adapted to serve Ethiopian parks \citep{Negesa2022}.

Yet despite these advantages, Ethiopian industrial parks today still rely on paper-based administration and fragmented communication. Service requests require in-person office visits. Approval processes offer little transparency. Fee collection involves cash handling or separate bank visits. These inefficiencies create delays, frustration, and missed opportunities for operational improvement \citep{Guteta2022, Guteta2023}.

\subsection{The Connectivity Challenge: Infrastructure Constraints}

Perhaps the most fundamental barrier to digital transformation in Ethiopian industrial parks is unreliable internet connectivity. Unlike their Chinese counterparts, which operate on stable high-speed networks, Ethiopian parks contend with regular outages that would render conventional cloud-based platforms unusable.

Survey respondents and published connectivity data confirm that Ethiopian parks typically lose internet for three to five hours daily, with complete outages occurring two to three times per month \citep{Ethiotelecom2023, ITU2023}. Infrastructure investment continues \citep{WorldBank2022}, but any near-term digital solution must work within these realities. During outages, a purely cloud-dependent system would simply be unavailable — potentially causing more disruption than the paper processes it was meant to replace.

This connectivity gap poses a serious adaptation challenge. Chinese platforms like WeChat Work and DingTalk assume persistent network access — an assumption that does not hold in the Ethiopian context. Deploying them without modification would lead to frequent downtime, user frustration, and almost certainly a reversion to manual processes.

\subsection{The Offline-First Paradigm: A Solution Framework}

The offline-first paradigm offers a compelling path forward. As \citet{Kleppmann2019} articulate in their influential work on local-first software, this paradigm flips the conventional assumption that being online is the default state. In an offline-first system, the application works fully without a network connection; connectivity becomes an enhancement, not a requirement.

It is worth distinguishing offline-first from merely offline-capable. An offline-capable system is designed primarily for online use, treating offline mode as a fallback — a reactive approach. An offline-first system, by contrast, treats offline operation as the primary design consideration — a proactive one. Given that Ethiopian parks lose connectivity for three to five hours daily and experience complete outages multiple times a month, offline is not an exceptional state; it is the expected one. This is why the present research adopts offline-first as its core design philosophy.

Progressive Web Application (PWA) technology provides the technical foundation for building offline-first web platforms. PWAs use service workers, client-side caching, and background synchronization to keep applications functional through connectivity disruptions \citep{AterPWA2017, Majchrzak2018, Tandel2018}. Research confirms that PWAs deliver experiences comparable to native apps while offering clear advantages in cross-platform deployment and offline capability \citep{AhnAllen2020, BiornHansen2017}. Crucially, a single PWA codebase serves desktop browsers, mobile browsers, and can be installed on phones as an app-like experience — eliminating the need for separate native mobile development.

Google's Firebase, and specifically its Firestore database, complements this approach with built-in offline persistence: data is automatically cached on-device and synchronized when the network returns \citep{GoogleFirebase2024}. Firebase's free tier (50,000 daily reads, 20,000 daily writes, 1~GB storage) comfortably covers prototype development and initial deployment. Combining Chinese smart park design thinking, offline-first PWA architecture, and Firebase backend services creates a genuine opportunity to build digital governance solutions that work where infrastructure is limited.

\section{Statement of the Problem}

IPDC operates multiple industrial parks serving domestic and international manufacturers. These parks depend on efficient service systems to support tenant operations — business licensing, facility management, utility services, maintenance coordination, and regulatory compliance. The quality of these services directly affects tenant satisfaction, operational efficiency, and the broader success of Ethiopia's industrial park programme.

Currently, IPDC relies almost entirely on paper-based systems and disconnected communication channels. Tenants visit administrative offices in person to file service requests, track progress through informal follow-ups, and navigate opaque bureaucratic processes. Several interconnected problems follow from this:

\textbf{Service Delivery Inefficiency:} Paper workflows slow request processing and approval routing. Requests get lost, misdirected, or stalled when staff are unavailable. Without automated tracking, neither staff nor tenants can reliably tell where any request stands.

\textbf{Fee Collection Challenges:} Fee collection is fragmented and lacks transparency. Cash payments raise security concerns; bank transfers require separate branch visits; and reconciling payments with services is a manual, error-prone process with no integrated system linking requests to payment status.

\textbf{Communication Gaps:} Information flows through multiple informal channels between management, administrative staff, operations teams, and tenants. Important announcements may not reach everyone, and there is no structured mechanism for tenant feedback.

\textbf{Operational Opacity:} Without digital records, management lacks the data needed to analyze service patterns, identify bottlenecks, or make informed resource allocation decisions.

\textbf{Stakeholder Frustration:} Tenant firms accustomed to digital services elsewhere find paper-based processes time-consuming and frustrating — an experience that affects retention and deters prospective investors.

Digitisation is the obvious answer, but conventional cloud-based platforms are a poor fit given the infrastructure constraints described above. Frequent outages would make cloud-dependent systems unavailable precisely when they are most needed --- a particularly troubling irony, since disruptions tend to cluster around periods of high operational activity. Existing research offers limited guidance for this specific challenge: the Chinese smart park literature assumes reliable connectivity \citep{Yang2023, Wang2022}; offline-first and PWA literature addresses simpler citizen services rather than complex industrial workflows \citep{Cherukuri2024, Muawwal2024}; and e-government research in developing countries acknowledges connectivity as a challenge without providing practical solutions for industrial settings \citep{Denbu2019, Lessa2019}.

This research fills that gap by building an offline-first digital one-stop service that brings Chinese smart park principles to the Ethiopian context. The central research question is: \textit{How can Chinese smart park digital governance models be systematically adapted using offline-first PWA architecture to deliver reliable digital services in connectivity-constrained Ethiopian industrial parks?}

\section{Motivations}

Several converging factors motivated this work:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Operational Efficiency:} Paper-based systems create bottlenecks across service delivery, approvals, and communication. A digital platform that consolidates these services — and keeps working during outages — would substantially reduce administrative burden and improve the tenant experience.

\item \textbf{Bridging the Connectivity Gap:} Most digital governance solutions assume stable internet, making them a poor fit for environments that lose connectivity for three to five hours daily. This research is grounded in the belief that reliable digital services are achievable even where infrastructure is limited.

\item \textbf{Leveraging China-Ethiopia Technology Relationships:} China's deep involvement in Ethiopian industrial parks creates a natural opportunity for technology adaptation. Platforms like WeChat Work and DingTalk offer tested governance models, but no systematic framework yet exists for adapting them to African infrastructure realities — and developing that framework is a core motivation for this work.

\item \textbf{Scalability and Replicability:} Ethiopia's industrial park programme is growing. A platform proven at existing parks could be rolled out across the entire IPDC portfolio, offering a cost-effective route to nationwide digital transformation through a single PWA codebase.

\item \textbf{Data-Driven Decision Making:} Paper-based systems produce no digital records. A digital platform with integrated AI capabilities — service classification, predictive maintenance, park recommendation — would fundamentally improve IPDC's capacity for evidence-based management.

\item \textbf{Contributing to ICT4D Knowledge:} Little research has applied offline-first principles to complex multi-stakeholder industrial workflows in developing countries. This work aims to contribute design principles and architectural patterns applicable to similar initiatives across Africa and other connectivity-constrained regions.
\end{itemize}

\section{Research Questions}

Three interconnected research questions guide this study, addressing the adaptation approach, the technical architecture, and the design principles for an effective offline-first platform.

\textbf{RQ1: What systematic adaptation methodology enables the transfer of Chinese Smart Park digital governance models to connectivity-constrained African industrial parks?}

This question addresses the conceptual challenge of taking systems designed for high-connectivity settings and adapting them for infrastructure-limited environments. It examines which elements of Chinese models can be transferred, what changes are necessary, and what approach should guide such adaptations.

\textbf{RQ2: How should offline-first PWA architecture be designed to maintain functional parity with online systems for complex industrial park service workflows?}

This question deals with the technical challenge of keeping industrial park services fully functional during connectivity disruptions — exploring caching strategies, synchronization mechanisms, conflict resolution, and user experience considerations for offline operation.

\textbf{RQ3: What design principles should guide feature prioritization for offline availability in multi-stakeholder industrial platforms?}

Not every feature can be equally critical or technically feasible for offline use. This question explores prioritization approaches that balance stakeholder needs with technical constraints.

\section{Research Objectives}

\subsection{General Objective}

The overall objective of this research is to design, develop, and evaluate an offline-first digital one-stop service Progressive Web Application (PWA) for the Ethiopian Industrial Parks Development Corporation (IPDC) by systematically adapting Chinese smart park digital governance models using Firebase backend services for connectivity-constrained environments.

\subsection{Specific Objectives}

To achieve this overall objective, the research pursues the following specific objectives:

\begin{enumerate}
\item To analyze and document existing service delivery processes and stakeholder requirements across IPDC industrial parks through comprehensive requirement gathering involving tenant firms, administrative staff, and operations personnel.

\item To develop a systematic adaptation framework identifying which elements of Chinese smart park models (WeChat Work, DingTalk) can be transferred and what modifications are needed for Ethiopian infrastructure constraints.

\item To design an offline-first system architecture incorporating PWA technologies (service workers, IndexedDB, background sync) together with Firebase backend services (Firestore with offline persistence, Authentication, Cloud Functions).

\item To build a functional PWA prototype accessible through web browsers on both desktop and mobile devices, demonstrating offline service request submission, digital service token-based fee tracking, local data persistence, automated synchronization, and role-based interfaces.

\item To evaluate the prototype through technical testing (offline functionality, synchronization accuracy, performance) and usability assessment with representative stakeholders.

\item To derive and document design principles for offline-first industrial service platforms based on the development experience and evaluation findings.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Significance of the Study}

This research contributes to both academic understanding and practical problem-solving across several domains.

\subsection{Academic Contributions}

\textbf{Technology Adaptation Framework:} A systematic methodology for adapting Chinese smart park models to connectivity-constrained African settings fills a gap in the literature — existing work documents Chinese implementations and general technology transfer principles separately, but no prior framework addresses this specific adaptation challenge.

\textbf{Offline-First Architecture for Complex Workflows:} By pushing offline-first PWA research beyond simple citizen services into complex multi-stakeholder industrial workflows, this work addresses the specific architectural challenges of approval processes, status tracking, and multi-party coordination in offline-capable systems.

\textbf{Design Science Research in African Contexts:} Applying DSR methodology to a practical problem in an African industrial setting adds to the growing body of literature focused on developing country applications.

\subsection{Practical Contributions}

\textbf{IPDC Digital Transformation:} The prototype directly addresses documented service delivery challenges at Ethiopian industrial parks, laying groundwork for broader digital transformation at IPDC.

\textbf{Replicable Solution Model:} The design principles and architecture patterns that emerge can serve as a template for similar projects in connectivity-constrained settings across Africa and beyond.

\textbf{Cross-Platform Accessibility:} The PWA approach demonstrates how one codebase serves both desktop and mobile users — a cost-effective path for organisations with limited development resources.

\subsection{Stakeholder Benefits}

\textbf{Tenant Enterprises:} Reliable digital service access regardless of connectivity, transparent fee structures, token tracking, and visibility into service request status.

\textbf{IPDC Administrative Staff:} Streamlined workflows, better tools for monitoring requests and payments, less paper handling, and a digital audit trail.

\textbf{Operations Personnel:} Improved maintenance and facility management coordination, with the ability to receive and update assignments even in the field without connectivity.

\textbf{IPDC Management:} Greater operational visibility, data supporting resource allocation, and improved efficiency across the park portfolio.

\section{Scope and Limitations}

\subsection{Scope of the Research}

\textbf{Geographic Scope:} The research targets IPDC-managed industrial parks in Ethiopia, drawing primary data from Bole Lemi and Hawassa Industrial Parks — two parks that differ in location, size, and tenant composition, providing a range of perspectives.

\textbf{Stakeholder Scope:} Three groups are involved: tenant firm representatives, IPDC administrative staff, and operations personnel.

\textbf{Functional Scope:} The prototype covers core service delivery functions: service request submission and tracking, maintenance request management, announcements, document management, and digital token-based fee tracking.

\textbf{Technical Scope---Platform Architecture:} The research produces a PWA built with modern web technologies, ensuring:
\begin{itemize}
\item Cross-platform accessibility across desktops, tablets, and phones without separate native applications;
\item Mobile app-like experience with home screen installation and offline access;
\item A single codebase serving all platforms consistently.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Technical Scope---Backend Services:} Firebase provides the cloud backend, chosen for its built-in offline persistence through Firestore.

\textbf{Fee Tracking Scope---Digital Service Token System:} Rather than integrating with external payment gateways, the platform uses an internal token system:
\begin{itemize}
\item Administrators assign tokens (credits) to tenant accounts;
\item Service fees are calculated and deducted from token balances;
\item When tenants pay through any external method, administrators add corresponding tokens.
\end{itemize}
This approach demonstrates the complete fee management workflow independently, with a clear path to future payment gateway integration.

\textbf{Scope Exclusions:} The research leaves out native mobile app development, external payment gateway integration, IoT sensor integration, and advanced analytics — all noted as directions for future work.

\subsection{Limitations}

\textbf{Prototype Stage:} The research produces a functional prototype rather than a production-ready system. Moving to full deployment would require additional security hardening and scalability work.

\textbf{Internal Token System:} While the token approach demonstrates the full fee management workflow, it requires manual administrator action when external payments come in. Integration with services like telebirr or CBE Birr is a future enhancement once the necessary API partnerships are in place.

\textbf{Evaluation Constraints:} The prototype is evaluated with a representative stakeholder group rather than through comprehensive testing across all parks.

\textbf{Connectivity Simulation:} Offline functionality testing necessarily uses simulated offline conditions rather than real network outages.

\textbf{Language Considerations:} The interface is in English, the main business language in Ethiopian industrial parks. Amharic localisation is noted as a future enhancement.

\section{Theoretical Framework}

This research rests on four complementary theoretical foundations.

\subsection{Design Science Research (DSR)}

Design Science Research serves as the overarching methodological framework. Following \citet{Hevner2004} and \citet{Peffers2007}, this research creates a purposeful IT artifact --- the offline-first digital platform --- while also generating design knowledge. The work adheres to Hevner's seven DSR guidelines: design as artifact, problem relevance, design evaluation, research contributions, research rigor, design as search process, and communication of research.

\subsection{Appropriate Technology Theory}

Rooted in \citet{Schumacher1973} and updated for the digital age by \citet{Toyama2015}, Appropriate Technology Theory insists that solutions must fit local conditions — infrastructure capabilities, available skills, economic resources, and existing ecosystems. The decision to use an internal token system rather than requiring external payment gateway partnerships is one concrete expression of this principle: it produces a self-contained solution that works within current organisational capabilities while remaining extensible.

\subsection{Local-First Software Principles}

As described by \citet{Kleppmann2019}, local-first principles put local data storage and processing first, treating network connectivity as an enhancement rather than a requirement. These principles directly shape key architecture decisions: using IndexedDB for client-side persistence, implementing service workers for request caching, and designing background synchronization strategies. Firebase Firestore's built-in offline persistence aligns naturally with this philosophy.

\subsection{Technology Transfer Framework}

Drawing on South-South technology transfer literature \citep{Chen2018, Hensengerth2018}, the research treats technology adaptation as an active, context-sensitive process rather than simple replication. The framework accounts for technological, organisational, cultural, and economic dimensions of the China-to-Ethiopia transfer.

\section{Definition of Key Terms}

\textbf{Offline-First Architecture:} A software design approach that treats local data storage and processing as primary, allowing full application functionality without a network connection. Network availability enhances synchronization but is not a prerequisite.

\textbf{Progressive Web Application (PWA):} A web application using service workers, web app manifests, and caching APIs to deliver app-like experiences with offline capability, push notifications, and home screen installation across desktop and mobile browsers.

\textbf{One-Stop Service (OSS):} A service delivery model that consolidates multiple services into a single access point, eliminating the need to visit separate channels or locations.

\textbf{Digital Service Token:} An internal unit of value used to track service fees within the platform. Tokens are assigned to tenant accounts by administrators and deducted when services are requested, providing fee transparency without requiring external payment integration.

\textbf{Firebase:} Google's Backend-as-a-Service platform offering Firestore (cloud database with offline persistence), authentication, hosting, and cloud functions.

\textbf{Service Worker:} A JavaScript file running in a separate browser thread that enables background processing, request interception, and caching strategies supporting offline functionality.

\textbf{IndexedDB:} A browser API for storing structured data client-side, supporting persistent local storage for offline-first applications.

\textbf{Smart Park:} An industrial park using digital technologies for intelligent management, automated services, real-time monitoring, and data-driven decision-making.

\textbf{Digital Governance:} The use of digital technologies to deliver organisational services, engage stakeholders, and manage operations electronically.

\textbf{Background Synchronization:} A PWA capability that automatically synchronizes data between client and server when connectivity is available, without user intervention.

\textbf{Connectivity-Constrained Environment:} A setting where internet access is unreliable, intermittent, or unavailable for extended periods, requiring special design consideration.

\textbf{Design Science Research (DSR):} A research paradigm that creates and evaluates IT artifacts to solve identified organisational problems, producing both practical solutions and generalizable knowledge.

\textbf{Industrial Parks Development Corporation (IPDC):} The Ethiopian government corporation, established in 2014, responsible for developing, managing, and operating industrial parks across Ethiopia.

\section{Chapter Summary}

Two things stand out from the context established in this chapter. First, the problem is real and consequential: paper-based administration in Ethiopian industrial parks imposes costs on tenants and IPDC alike, and the connectivity constraints that make conventional digital solutions impractical are not temporary conditions that will resolve themselves quickly. Second, the opportunity is genuine: Chinese smart park platforms have demonstrated what digital park governance can look like, and the China-Ethiopia industrial relationships create a practical pathway for knowledge transfer --- if the transfer is done thoughtfully. Three research questions and six objectives frame how this research approaches that opportunity, grounded in a theoretical framework drawing on Design Science Research, Appropriate Technology Theory, Local-First Software Principles, and Technology Transfer Theory. Chapter~\ref{ch:literature} reviews the literature these frameworks build on and sharpens the specific gap this research fills.
