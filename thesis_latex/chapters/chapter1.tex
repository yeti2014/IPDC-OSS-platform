\section{Background of the Study}

\subsection{Global Context: Digital Transformation in Industrial Parks}

Industrial park management around the world is going through a significant digital shift. Parks are increasingly turning to smart technologies to run operations more efficiently, deliver better services, and give their tenant enterprises a competitive edge. At the heart of this shift are digital platforms that bring administrative services together, support real-time communication, and enable decisions grounded in data \citep{Yang2025, Huang2023}.

China stands out as a frontrunner in smart industrial park development. Chinese parks have built comprehensive digital ecosystems---largely powered by platforms like WeChat Work and DingTalk---that reshape how parks and tenants interact. These platforms offer integrated one-stop services covering business registration, permit processing, facility management, and stakeholder communication \citep{Huang2023, Schreieck2022}. They illustrate how digital governance can make park operations smoother and improve the tenant experience.

The success of these Chinese models has sparked interest in bringing similar approaches to other settings, particularly in developing economies that want to strengthen the competitiveness of their industrial zones. The Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) and its digital counterpart, the Digital Silk Road (DSR), have established pathways for technology exchange between China and partner countries, including those across Africa \citep{LiuBRI2023, deKluiver2024, ElKadi2024}.

\subsection{Ethiopian Context: Industrial Parks and Digital Transformation}

Ethiopia has invested heavily in industrial park development as a pillar of its economic transformation strategy \citep{Oqubay2015}. The Industrial Parks Development Corporation (IPDC), set up in 2014, manages a portfolio of parks throughout the country, including Bole Lemi Industrial Park, Eastern Industrial Zone, Hawassa Industrial Park, and several others \citep{IPDC2020}. These parks host both domestic and international manufacturers and play a meaningful role in generating export revenue and creating jobs \citep{Guteta2024, Tesfaw2023}.

The connection between Ethiopia and China in this space is especially notable. A number of Ethiopian industrial parks were built with Chinese investment and technical support, and Chinese firms make up a significant share of park tenants. This relationship opens up both an opportunity and a practical justification for examining how Chinese digital governance approaches might be adapted for use in Ethiopian parks \citep{Negesa2022}.

Yet Ethiopian industrial parks today still rely largely on paper-based administration and fragmented communication channels. Submitting a service request means visiting an office in person. Approval processes lack transparency. Fee collection involves cash handling or separate bank visits. These inefficiencies create delays, frustration, and missed chances to improve operations \citep{Guteta2022, Guteta2023}.

\subsection{The Connectivity Challenge: Infrastructure Constraints}

One of the most fundamental barriers to going digital in Ethiopian industrial parks is unreliable internet. Unlike their Chinese counterparts, which enjoy stable high-speed connectivity, Ethiopian parks deal with regular internet outages that would make conventional cloud-based platforms unusable.

Field observations and conversations with stakeholders indicate that Ethiopian parks typically lose internet for three to five hours each day, with complete outages happening two to three times per month \citep{Ethiotelecom2023, ITU2023}. Although infrastructure investment continues \citep{WorldBank2022}, any near-term digital solution must work within these constraints. During disruption periods, a purely cloud-dependent system would simply be unavailable---potentially causing more disruption than the paper processes it was meant to replace.

This connectivity gap poses a serious adaptation challenge. Chinese platforms like WeChat Work and DingTalk assume the network is always there---an assumption that simply does not hold in the Ethiopian context. Deploying these platforms without modification would lead to frequent downtime, user frustration, and most likely a return to traditional manual processes.

\subsection{The Offline-First Paradigm: A Solution Framework}

The offline-first paradigm presents a compelling way to address these connectivity constraints. As articulated by \citet{Kleppmann2019} in their influential work on local-first software, this paradigm flips the conventional assumption that being online is the default state. Instead, offline-first systems are built to work fully without a network connection, and connectivity is treated as a bonus rather than a necessity.

It is worth distinguishing between offline-first and offline-capable. An offline-capable system is designed primarily for online use, with offline mode as a fallback---essentially a reactive approach. An offline-first system, by contrast, treats offline operation as the primary design consideration, with online connectivity as an enhancement---a proactive approach. Since Ethiopian industrial parks face three to five hours of daily disruption and complete outages multiple times a month, being offline is not exceptional; it is the expected condition. For this reason, the present research adopts offline-first as its core design philosophy, and the term ``offline-first'' in the thesis title reflects this architectural commitment.

Progressive Web Application (PWA) technology provides the technical foundation for building offline-first web platforms. PWAs use service workers, client-side caching, and background synchronization to keep applications working through connectivity disruptions \citep{AterPWA2017, Majchrzak2018, Tandel2018}. Studies confirm that PWAs can deliver user experiences on par with native apps while offering clear advantages in cross-platform deployment and offline capability \citep{AhnAllen2020, BiornHansen2017}. A notable benefit of the PWA approach is that one codebase serves desktop browsers, mobile browsers, and can even be installed on phones as an app-like experience---no separate native mobile development required.

Cloud platforms have also evolved to support offline-first designs. Google's Firebase, and specifically its Firestore database, offers built-in offline persistence: data is automatically cached on the device and synchronized when the network returns \citep{GoogleFirebase2024}. This greatly reduces the engineering effort needed for offline-first applications while still providing enterprise-grade services like authentication, real-time sync, and cloud functions. Firebase's free tier (50,000 daily reads, 20,000 daily writes, 1GB storage) covers prototype development and initial deployment comfortably, making it well-suited for thesis research and pilot use.

Bringing together Chinese smart park design thinking, offline-first PWA architecture, and Firebase backend services creates an opportunity to build digital solutions that actually work in connectivity-constrained environments. This research explores that combination by developing an offline-first digital one-stop-shop platform for Ethiopian IPDC, showing how advanced digital governance models can be made to work where infrastructure is limited.

\section{Statement of the Problem}

The Ethiopian Industrial Parks Development Corporation (IPDC) operates multiple industrial parks that serve domestic and international manufacturers. These parks need efficient service systems to support tenant operations---business licensing, facility management, utility services, maintenance coordination, and regulatory compliance. How well these services are delivered directly affects tenant satisfaction, operational efficiency, and ultimately the success of the industrial park program.

At present, IPDC relies mostly on paper-based systems and disconnected communication channels. Tenants have to go to administrative offices in person to file service requests. They track request status through informal follow-ups. They navigate bureaucratic processes with little visibility into what is happening. This situation gives rise to several interconnected problems:

\textbf{Service Delivery Inefficiency:} Paper-based workflows slow down request processing, approval routing, and service completion. Requests can get lost, sent to the wrong person, or stalled when staff are unavailable. Without automated tracking, neither staff nor tenants can reliably tell where a request stands.

\textbf{Fee Collection Challenges:} Fee collection is fragmented and lacks transparency. Cash payments raise security and accountability concerns. Bank transfers force tenants to visit branches separately, and reconciling payments with services is a manual, error-prone process. There is no integrated system connecting service requests to payment status.

\textbf{Communication Gaps:} Information flows between park management, administrative staff, operations teams, and tenants through multiple informal channels. Important announcements may not reach everyone, and there is no structured way for tenants to report concerns or provide feedback.

\textbf{Operational Opacity:} Without digital records, management has no data to work with when analyzing service patterns, spotting bottlenecks, or deciding how to allocate resources.

\textbf{Stakeholder Frustration:} Tenant enterprises that are accustomed to digital services elsewhere find the paper-based processes time-consuming and frustrating. This frustration can affect tenant retention and deter prospective investors.

While going digital is an obvious solution direction, conventional cloud-based platforms are a poor fit for the Ethiopian context because of the infrastructure constraints described above. Frequent connectivity disruptions would make cloud-dependent systems unavailable precisely when they are most needed.

Existing research offers limited guidance for this specific challenge. The Chinese smart park literature describes sophisticated digital governance systems but assumes reliable connectivity \citep{Yang2023, Wang2022}. The offline-first and PWA literature demonstrates techniques for handling connectivity issues but mostly addresses simple citizen services rather than complex industrial workflows \citep{Cherukuri2024, Muawwal2024}. E-government research in developing countries recognizes connectivity as a challenge but provides few practical solutions for industrial settings \citep{Denbu2019, Lessa2019}.

This research fills the gap by building an offline-first digital one-stop-shop that brings Chinese smart park principles to the Ethiopian context. The central research question is: \textit{How can Chinese smart park digital governance models be systematically adapted using offline-first PWA architecture to deliver reliable digital services in connectivity-constrained Ethiopian industrial parks?}

\section{Motivations}

Several converging factors motivate the development of an offline-first digital one-stop-shop for Ethiopian industrial parks. The main reasons for undertaking this study are as follows:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Operational Efficiency and Service Delivery:} Ethiopian industrial parks currently depend on paper-based systems that create bottlenecks in service delivery, approvals, and communication. Tenants spend too much time dealing with manual processes, which cuts into their productivity. A digital platform that brings these services together in one interface---and keeps working even when the internet goes down---would substantially reduce administrative burden and improve the tenant experience.

\item \textbf{Bridging the Connectivity Gap:} Most existing digital governance solutions assume stable internet access, making them a poor fit for Ethiopian parks that lose connectivity for three to five hours daily. There is a clear need for solutions that treat offline operation as a fundamental design requirement, not an afterthought. This research is driven by the belief that reliable digital services are possible even where connectivity is limited.

\item \textbf{Leveraging China-Ethiopia Technology Relationships:} China's deep involvement in building, investing in, and tenanting Ethiopian industrial parks creates a natural opportunity for technology adaptation. Platforms like WeChat Work and DingTalk offer tested models for digital park governance, but no systematic framework exists for adapting them to African infrastructure realities. Developing such a framework is a key motivation for this work.

\item \textbf{Scalability and Replicability:} Ethiopia's industrial park program is growing, with new parks under development. A digital platform that proves itself at existing parks could be rolled out across the entire IPDC portfolio, offering a cost-effective route to nationwide digital transformation. The PWA architecture means one codebase serves desktop and mobile users at every park, with no need for separate native apps.

\item \textbf{Data-Driven Decision Making:} The current paper-based system produces no digital records, leaving park management without the information needed for sound decisions about resource allocation, bottleneck identification, and process improvement. A digital platform with AI capabilities---service classification, predictive maintenance, park recommendation---would fundamentally improve IPDC's capacity for evidence-based management.

\item \textbf{Contributing to ICT4D Knowledge:} Although interest in offline-first and local-first software is growing, little research has applied these ideas to complex multi-stakeholder industrial workflows in developing countries. This research aims to contribute design principles and architectural patterns that can inform similar digital transformation efforts across Africa and other connectivity-constrained regions.
\end{itemize}

\section{Research Questions}

Three interconnected research questions guide this study, addressing the adaptation approach, the technical architecture, and the design principles needed for an effective offline-first platform for Ethiopian industrial parks.

\textbf{RQ1: What systematic adaptation methodology enables the transfer of Chinese Smart Park digital governance models to connectivity-constrained African industrial parks?}

This question tackles the conceptual and methodological challenge of taking sophisticated digital systems designed for high-connectivity settings and adapting them for environments with infrastructure limitations. It examines which elements of Chinese models can be transferred, what changes are necessary, and what systematic approach should guide such adaptations.

\textbf{RQ2: How should offline-first PWA architecture be designed to maintain functional parity with online systems for complex industrial park service workflows?}

This question deals with the technical challenge of building industrial park services that remain fully usable during connectivity disruptions. It explores caching strategies, synchronization mechanisms, conflict resolution approaches, and user experience considerations for offline operation.

\textbf{RQ3: What design principles should guide feature prioritization for offline availability in multi-stakeholder industrial platforms?}

This question addresses the practical design challenge of deciding which features need to work offline and which ones can reasonably require connectivity. Since not every feature may be equally critical or technically feasible for offline use, the question explores prioritization approaches that balance what stakeholders need with what is technically possible.

\section{Research Objectives}

\subsection{General Objective}

The overall objective of this research is to design, develop, and evaluate an offline-first digital one-stop-shop Progressive Web Application (PWA) for the Ethiopian Industrial Parks Development Corporation (IPDC) by systematically adapting Chinese smart park digital governance models using Firebase backend services for connectivity-constrained environments.

\subsection{Specific Objectives}

To achieve this overall objective, the research pursues the following specific objectives:

\begin{enumerate}
\item To analyze and document existing service delivery processes and stakeholder requirements across IPDC industrial parks through comprehensive requirement gathering involving tenant firms, administrative staff, and operations personnel.

\item To develop a systematic adaptation framework that identifies which elements of Chinese smart park models (WeChat Work, DingTalk) can be transferred and what modifications are needed for Ethiopian infrastructure constraints.

\item To design an offline-first system architecture that incorporates PWA technologies (service workers, IndexedDB, background sync) together with Firebase backend services (Firestore with offline persistence, Authentication, Cloud Functions).

\item To build a functional PWA prototype accessible through web browsers on both desktop and mobile devices, demonstrating key capabilities including offline service request submission, digital service token-based fee tracking, local data persistence, automated synchronization, and role-based interfaces for different stakeholders.

\item To evaluate the prototype through technical testing (offline functionality verification, synchronization accuracy, performance measurement) and usability assessment with representative stakeholders.

\item To derive and document design principles for offline-first industrial service platforms based on the development experience and evaluation findings, producing knowledge that can be applied to similar projects elsewhere.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Significance of the Study}

This research contributes to both academic understanding and practical problem-solving across several domains. Its significance spans theoretical, methodological, and practical dimensions.

\subsection{Academic Contributions}

\textbf{Technology Adaptation Framework:} This research produces a systematic methodology for adapting Chinese smart park digital governance models for connectivity-constrained African industrial parks. Although existing literature documents Chinese implementations and general technology transfer principles separately, no prior work offers a concrete framework for this particular adaptation challenge.

\textbf{Offline-First Architecture for Complex Workflows:} The research pushes offline-first PWA work beyond simple citizen services into the territory of complex multi-stakeholder industrial workflows. It specifically tackles the architectural challenges of implementing approval processes, status tracking, and multi-party coordination in systems that must work offline.

\textbf{Design Science Research in African Contexts:} By applying Design Science Research methodology to a practical problem in an African industrial setting, this work adds to the growing body of DSR literature focused on developing country contexts.

\subsection{Practical Contributions}

\textbf{IPDC Digital Transformation:} The most direct practical contribution is a prototype digital platform that addresses the service delivery challenges documented in Ethiopian industrial parks. The platform lays groundwork for broader digital transformation efforts at IPDC.

\textbf{Replicable Solution Model:} The design principles and architecture patterns that emerge from this research can serve as a template for similar digital transformation projects in other connectivity-constrained settings across Africa and the developing world.

\textbf{Cross-Platform Accessibility:} The PWA approach shows how one codebase can serve both desktop and mobile users, offering a cost-effective path for organizations that have limited development resources.

\subsection{Stakeholder Benefits}

\textbf{Tenant Enterprises:} Reliable access to digital services regardless of connectivity, transparent fee structures, digital token tracking, and better visibility into the status of service requests.

\textbf{IPDC Administrative Staff:} Smoother workflows, better tools for monitoring service requests and payment status, less paper handling, and digital records that provide an audit trail.

\textbf{Operations Personnel:} Better coordination for maintenance and facility management, with the ability to receive and update work assignments even in the field without connectivity.

\textbf{IPDC Management:} Greater visibility into park operations, data that supports resource allocation decisions, and improved operational efficiency.

\section{Scope and Limitations}

\subsection{Scope of the Research}

This research covers the following elements:

\textbf{Geographic Scope:} The research targets IPDC-managed industrial parks in Ethiopia, drawing primary data from Bole Lemi Industrial Park and Hawassa Industrial Park. These two parks differ in location, size, and tenant composition, providing a range of perspectives on requirements and constraints.

\textbf{Stakeholder Scope:} Three primary stakeholder groups are involved: tenant firm representatives (operations managers, administrative staff), IPDC administrative staff (service coordinators, permit officers, finance officers), and operations personnel (facility managers, maintenance coordinators).

\textbf{Functional Scope:} The prototype covers core service delivery functions: service request submission and tracking, maintenance request management, announcement and notification distribution, basic document management, and digital service token-based fee tracking.

\textbf{Technical Scope---Platform Architecture:} The research produces a Progressive Web Application (PWA) built with modern web technologies. The PWA architecture ensures:
\begin{itemize}
\item Cross-platform accessibility---the platform works in standard web browsers on desktops, tablets, and phones without requiring separate native applications;
\item Mobile app-like experience---on mobile devices, users can install the PWA to their home screen for full-screen operation and offline access;
\item Single codebase---one codebase serves all platforms, keeping development manageable and ensuring consistent behavior across devices.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Technical Scope---Backend Services:} Firebase provides the cloud backend, chosen for its built-in offline persistence through Firestore. The free tier offers enough capacity for prototype development and pilot testing.

\textbf{Fee Tracking Scope---Digital Service Token System:} Rather than integrating with external payment gateways, the platform uses an internal digital service token system for fee tracking. In this system:
\begin{itemize}
\item Administrators assign tokens (credits) to tenant accounts;
\item Service fees are calculated and deducted from token balances;
\item When tenants pay through any external method (cash, bank transfer, mobile money), administrators add corresponding tokens to their accounts.
\end{itemize}

This approach demonstrates the complete fee management workflow, works independently without external payment API partnerships, and provides a foundation that can later be extended with external payment integration.

\textbf{Scope Exclusions:} The research explicitly leaves out:
\begin{itemize}
\item Native mobile app development (Android APK or iOS app)---the PWA adequately covers mobile access needs;
\item External payment gateway integration (telebirr, CBE Birr, etc.)---the internal token system provides an equivalent workflow demonstration;
\item IoT sensor integration---while common in Chinese smart parks, this goes beyond what a thesis can reasonably cover;
\item Advanced analytics and AI features---basic reporting is included; more advanced analytics is noted as future work.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Limitations}

This research acknowledges the following limitations:

\textbf{Prototype Stage:} What the research produces is a functional prototype, not a production-ready system. While the prototype demonstrates core capabilities, bringing it to full deployment would require additional work on security hardening and scalability.

\textbf{Internal Token System:} The prototype tracks fees through an internal token system rather than connecting to external payment platforms. While this demonstrates the full fee management workflow and the architecture is designed to be extensible, it does require administrators to manually credit tokens when external payments come in. Integration with payment services like telebirr or CBE Birr is identified as a future enhancement once the necessary partnerships and API access are in place.

\textbf{Evaluation Constraints:} The prototype is evaluated with a limited group of representative stakeholders rather than through comprehensive testing across all parks.

\textbf{Connectivity Simulation:} Although the prototype is designed for real connectivity challenges, testing of offline functionality necessarily uses simulated offline conditions.

\textbf{Language Considerations:} The interface is developed in English, which is the main business language in Ethiopian industrial parks. Full localization to Amharic and other Ethiopian languages is noted as a future enhancement.

\section{Theoretical Framework}

This research rests on four complementary theoretical foundations that inform the methodology, design decisions, and interpretation of findings.

\subsection{Design Science Research (DSR)}

Design Science Research serves as the overarching methodological framework. Following \citet{Hevner2004} and \citet{Peffers2007}, this research creates a purposeful IT artifact---the offline-first digital platform---while also generating knowledge about the design process and the principles behind it. The work adheres to Hevner's seven DSR guidelines: design as artifact, problem relevance, design evaluation, research contributions, research rigor, design as search process, and communication of research.

\subsection{Appropriate Technology Theory}

Appropriate Technology Theory, rooted in the work of \citet{Schumacher1973} and updated for the digital age by \citet{Toyama2015}, provides the conceptual lens for technology adaptation decisions. The central idea is that technology solutions need to fit local conditions---infrastructure capabilities, available skills, economic resources, and existing ecosystems. The decision to use an internal token system rather than requiring external payment gateway partnerships is one example of this principle at work: it produces a self-contained solution that functions within current organizational capabilities while remaining open to future extension.

\subsection{Local-First Software Principles}

The local-first software paradigm, as described by \citet{Kleppmann2019}, supplies the technical design philosophy for the platform. Local-first principles put local data storage and processing first, treating network connectivity as an enhancement rather than a requirement. These principles directly shape key architectural decisions: using IndexedDB for client-side persistence, implementing service workers for request caching, and designing background synchronization strategies. Firebase Firestore's built-in offline persistence aligns naturally with local-first principles, since it maintains a local data cache and handles synchronization transparently.

\subsection{Technology Transfer Framework}

Technology transfer theory guides the adaptation of Chinese smart park models to the Ethiopian setting. Drawing on South-South technology transfer literature \citep{Chen2018, Hensengerth2018}, the research acknowledges that successful transfer requires active adaptation, not simple replication. The framework takes into account technological, organizational, cultural, and economic dimensions.

\section{Definition of Key Terms}

The following definitions clarify how key terms are used throughout this research:

\textbf{Offline-First Architecture:} A software design approach that puts local data storage and processing first, allowing full application functionality without a network connection. Network availability is treated as an enhancement for synchronization, not a prerequisite.

\textbf{Progressive Web Application (PWA):} A web application that uses modern technologies---service workers, web app manifests, and caching APIs---to deliver app-like experiences with offline capabilities, push notifications, and home screen installation. PWAs work in web browsers on both desktop and mobile devices.

\textbf{One-Stop-Shop (OSS):} A service delivery model that brings multiple services together into a single access point, so users do not have to visit multiple channels or locations.

\textbf{Digital Service Token:} An internal unit of value used within the platform to track service fees. Tokens are assigned to tenant accounts by administrators and deducted when services are requested, providing fee transparency without requiring external payment gateway integration.

\textbf{Firebase:} Google's Backend-as-a-Service (BaaS) platform offering a cloud database (Firestore), authentication, hosting, and cloud functions. Firestore's built-in offline persistence caches data locally and synchronizes with the cloud when connectivity returns.

\textbf{Service Worker:} A JavaScript file that runs in a separate thread from the main browser, enabling background processing, request interception, and caching strategies that support offline functionality.

\textbf{IndexedDB:} A low-level browser API for storing significant amounts of structured data on the client side, supporting persistent local storage for offline-first applications.

\textbf{Smart Park:} An industrial park that uses digital technologies for intelligent management, automated services, real-time monitoring, and data-driven decision-making.

\textbf{Digital Governance:} The use of digital technologies to deliver organizational services, engage stakeholders, and manage operations electronically.

\textbf{Background Synchronization:} A PWA capability that automatically synchronizes data between client and server when connectivity is available, without requiring the user to do anything.

\textbf{Connectivity-Constrained Environment:} A setting where internet access is unreliable, intermittent, or unavailable for extended periods, requiring special consideration in how digital systems are designed.

\textbf{Design Science Research (DSR):} A research paradigm that creates and evaluates IT artifacts to solve identified organizational problems, producing both practical solutions and knowledge that can be generalized.

\textbf{Industrial Parks Development Corporation (IPDC):} The Ethiopian government corporation, established in 2014, that is responsible for developing, managing, and operating industrial parks across Ethiopia.
